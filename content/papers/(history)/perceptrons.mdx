**Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry** (Marvin
Minsky and Seymour Papert, 1969) is not a research paper but a book — and
one of the most consequential publications in the field's history
regardless, for what it took away rather than what it added: it's among
the publications most often credited with triggering the first AI winter
for neural networks, a credit its authors later disputed.

## What problem it solved

By the late 1960s, [Frank Rosenblatt](/people/frank-rosenblatt)'s
[perceptron](/reference/perceptron) had generated significant hype — press
coverage suggested machines that could see, speak, and think were close
at hand. Minsky and Papert set out to answer a narrower, more rigorous
question that the hype had outrun: *what, precisely, can a single-layer
perceptron actually compute?*

## The key idea

Prove it mathematically rather than argue it informally. The book's
central result shows that a single-layer perceptron can only learn
functions that are **linearly separable** — representable by a single
straight-line (or hyperplane) decision boundary — and demonstrates that
even a function as simple as **XOR** falls outside that class. No amount
of training data or training time lets a single-layer perceptron learn
XOR, because the limitation is structural, not a matter of optimization.
XOR is the easiest illustration; the book's wider results, on properties
like parity and connectedness under locality restrictions, showed the
limits ran deeper than one function.

<Misconception claim="Minsky and Papert showed neural networks fundamentally can't work.">
  The book's theorems concern single-layer perceptrons; networks with
  hidden layers sat outside them. The authors were nonetheless
  pessimistic that multi-layer versions would be trainable or useful
  (a later edition called such extensions sterile), so the book didn't
  point the way out. What the field lacked was a way to *train* a
  multi-layer network; the algorithm that made that practical,
  [backpropagation](/reference/backpropagation), was over a decade away.
  The result was read as a broader case against neural networks as a
  research direction, not just against the single-layer case the math
  actually covered.
</Misconception>

## Why it mattered

Funding for connectionist (neural network) research dried up for most of
a decade after the book's publication — the period historians call the
first AI winter for neural networks specifically, as research attention
shifted toward symbolic AI approaches. How much of that is the book's
doing is debated: Minsky and Papert later disputed it, and the shift also
reflected a broader souring on AI promises. What isn't debated is the
irony the field returned to repeatedly afterward: the book's proofs were
correct, and the eventual fix, hidden layers trained with backpropagation,
lay just outside what they covered. It's a standing
reminder that a rigorous negative result about one specific architecture
can be misread as a verdict on an entire research direction — and that
[Marvin Minsky](/people/marvin-minsky), one of the field's founders, is
also the person most associated with nearly ending one of its most
important sub-fields before it had the tools to succeed.
