**Marvin Minsky** (1927–2016) co-founded the MIT Artificial Intelligence
Laboratory in 1959 and spent the next five decades arguing, in books,
papers, and famously combative talks, about what intelligence actually is
and whether it could be built from parts. This site is named for him.

His most consequential technical work is also his most controversial. In
1969, with Seymour Papert, Minsky published *Perceptrons*, a rigorous
mathematical analysis of [Frank Rosenblatt](/people/frank-rosenblatt)'s
single-layer [perceptron](/reference/perceptron). The book proved these
networks couldn't represent even simple functions like XOR — correct, and
already understood to be fixable by adding hidden layers, but the book was
read (fairly or not) as a broader case against neural networks entirely.
Funding for connectionist research dried up for most of a decade, a
period historians call the first AI winter. Minsky spent his later career
mostly on symbolic approaches, developing "frames" — structured
representations of everyday knowledge — and in 1986 published
*The Society of Mind*, arguing that intelligence emerges from many
simple, specialized processes acting together rather than one unified
mechanism, an idea with clear echoes in how modern
[agent](/reference/agent) systems are built.

He remained skeptical that scaling up existing methods, connectionist or
otherwise, would be sufficient for general intelligence — a debate that
is, in different language, still the live disagreement between scaling
optimists and skeptics today.
